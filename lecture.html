Part 1

What is a moduli space?

A moduli space is a geometric space whose points are in bijection with the isomorphism classes of some kind of mathematical object. Think of it as a menu of your objects: picking a point of the space picks out one of your items. Moduli spaces have a few key features: moving continuously in the space deforms the object continuously, and the boundary of the space tends to hold degenerate versions of the object. We start with two warm-up examples before turning to graphs.

Example: segments in ℝ2

A segment in the plane is a set [p, q] = {(1 − t)p + tq : 0 ≤ t ≤ 1} for two distinct points p and q. Call two segments equivalent if some rotation followed by a translation carries one onto the other. What does the space of all segments look like?

The strategy is to pick out, from each equivalence class, one representative of a specific form. Translate any segment so that p sits at the origin, then rotate it so that q lies on the positive x-axis. Using this translation and rotation, every segment is equivalent to one of the form [(0, 0), (ℓ, 0)], where ℓ = |q − p| > 0. Rotations and translations preserve length, so different values of ℓ give segments that are not equivalent. The length is the only parameter:

{segments in ℝ2} / (rotations, translations)≅(0, ∞), [p, q] ↦ |q − p|.

What happens as ℓ approaches 0? The two endpoints collide and the segment collapses to a single point. That point is no longer a segment, but it is the natural limit of segments: a degenerate segment. It sits at the boundary point 0 of the ray, and adding it closes up (0, ∞) to [0, ∞).

Every segment also has a symmetry that swaps its two endpoints: the half-turn about its midpoint. That symmetry does not change ℓ, so it leaves no trace in the picture. The same thing will happen with loops in Part 4.

Why this example? Segments are the building block for the rest of the lecture. An edge of a metric graph is a segment, determined up to isometry by its length, and length zero is exactly where the edge degenerates. A graph with k edges will contribute a copy of (0, ∞)k, one coordinate per edge.

Example: lines through the origin

Let's make a moduli space for the set of lines through the origin in ℝ2. A first idea is to use the slope: the line y = mx goes to the number m. This doesn't work too well. The vertical line has no slope, and lines that are close to vertical have huge positive or huge negative slopes, so lines that are close together can be very far apart in ℝ. A better parameter is the angle θ that the line makes with the x-axis, taken in [0, π).

As θ approaches π the line comes back to the x-axis, so the two ends of the interval [0, π) are glued together and the space of lines is a circle. To make this precise, send the line at angle θ to the point (cos 2θ, sin 2θ). This is a map from the set of lines through the origin to the unit circle in ℝ2, and it is a bijection: doubling the angle stretches the half-turn [0, π) once around the full circle.

Three things to remember

  1. Points of a moduli space are isomorphism classes of objects.
  2. Moving continuously in a moduli space deforms the object continuously.
  3. The boundary holds degenerations, where something shrinks to zero.
Part 2

Graphs, rigorously

The definition of a graph as a vertex set with a set of two-element subsets has trouble with three things we need: loops, several edges between the same two vertices, and edges that hang off a vertex and go nowhere. Half-edges handle all three at once.

Definition · graph

A graph G = (V, H, r, ι) consists of

  • a finite set V of vertices,
  • a finite set H of half-edges,
  • a map r : H → V, the root map, attaching each half-edge to a vertex,
  • an involution ι : H → H, meaning ι ∘ ι = id.

If ι(h) ≠ h, then h and ι(h) form an edge: the two halves glue into one edge between r(h) and r(ι(h)). If ι(h) = h, then h is a leg: a half-edge that hangs off a vertex and goes nowhere.

A loop is an edge whose two halves have the same root. Parallel edges are several edges whose halves have the same pair of roots. Neither needs a special case.

We add two decorations.

  1. Marked legs. A bijection m from the set L of legs to {1, 2, …, n}. We draw each leg with its number at the end.
  2. Vertex weights. A function w : V → {0, 1, 2, …}. We draw a weight as a number inside the vertex and leave weight-0 vertices as plain dots.
Definition · genus

The genus of a connected graph G is

g(G) = |E| − |V| + 1 + ∑v ∈ V w(v).

The number |E| − |V| + 1 is denoted b1(G) and counts the independent cycles of G: it is the number of edges outside a spanning tree. The weights count genus that is hidden at a vertex. Contraction, at the end of this part, explains where hidden genus comes from.

Definition · stable

A graph G is stable if every vertex satisfies

2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| > 0.

Here r−1(v) is the set of half-edges rooted at v, so |r−1(v)| counts them. A loop at v contributes two half-edges and a leg contributes one. For a graph with no loops or legs, |r−1(v)| is the usual degree of v.

Reading the table: a weight-0 vertex needs at least 3 half-edges, a weight-1 vertex needs at least 1, and a vertex of weight 2 or more is always fine. So a stable graph has no unweighted vertex with 1 or 2 half-edges. Those are the leaves and the vertices in the middle of an edge, which carry no information.

Before creating a moduli space for graphs, we need to know when two graphs should be considered equivalent.

Definition · isomorphism

An isomorphism G → G′ is a pair of bijections fV : V → V′ and fH : H → H′ that respect all of the structure:

  • roots: if h is rooted at v, then fH(h) is rooted at fV(v). In symbols, r′ ∘ fH = fV ∘ r.
  • pairing: if h and k form an edge, then fH(h) and fH(k) form an edge, and legs go to legs. In symbols, ι′ ∘ fH = fH ∘ ι.
  • weights: v and fV(v) have the same weight. In symbols, w′ ∘ fV = w.
  • leg labels: the leg labelled i goes to the leg labelled i. In symbols, m′ ∘ fH = m on legs.

The isomorphisms G → G form the group Aut(G).

In the loop-and-leg graph in the figure, swapping the half-edges a and b and fixing everything else is an automorphism. So |Aut(G)| = 2, even though no vertex and no edge moves. The old definition of a graph cannot see this symmetry, which is one reason half-edges are the right language.

Why insist on stability? Without it, we could keep adding new vertices in the middle of an edge, or keep attaching new edges that end at a new bare vertex (a leaf). Each of these moves adds one vertex and one edge, so |E| − |V| + 1 and the genus do not change. It adds no legs either: a leaf is a vertex, not a leg. So there would be infinitely many graphs with the same genus and the same number of legs. Stability rules this out.

Proposition

For each g and n, there are finitely many connected stable graphs of genus g with n marked legs, up to isomorphism.

Proof

We prove that such a graph has at most 2g − 2 + n vertices and at most 3g − 3 + n edges. Finiteness follows from these bounds, as explained at the end.

Vertices. Counting each vertex once and then using stability at every vertex,

|V|=∑v1
≤∑v( 2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| )
=2∑vw(v) − 2|V| + 2|E| + n
=2g − 2 + n.

Point at a coloured term, or at a sign, to see where it comes from.

This proves |V| ≤ 2g − 2 + n. Since there is at least one vertex, it also shows that 2g − 2 + n > 0 whenever such a graph exists.

Edges. Since g = |E| − |V| + 1 + ∑w(v), we get

|E|=g − ∑vw(v) + |V| − 1
≤g + 0 + (2g − 2 + n) − 1
=3g − 3 + n.

Point at a coloured term, or at a sign, to see where it comes from.

When does equality hold? The edge bound is an equality exactly when both of its inputs are: every weight is 0, and |V| = 2g − 2 + n. The vertex bound is an equality exactly when every term 2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| that replaced a 1 is equal to 1. With w(v) = 0 this says |r−1(v)| − 2 = 1, so every vertex has exactly 3 half-edges. We call such a vertex trivalent. Finally, with |V| and |E| bounded, |H| = 2|E| + n is bounded and every weight is at most g, so there are only finitely many possibilities for (V, H, r, ι, w, m). ∎

Edge contraction

Definition · contraction

For an edge e of G, the contraction G/e is defined as follows.

  • If e joins distinct vertices u and v, merge them into one vertex of weight w(u) + w(v).
  • If e is a loop at v, delete it and increase w(v) by 1.

Everything else stays as it was: the marked legs keep their labels, the other edges keep their ends, and anything that was attached to u or v is now attached to the merged vertex.

Two key facts · check them in the figure

  1. Contraction preserves genus. Contracting a loop lowers |E| − |V| + 1 by 1 and raises a weight by 1. Contracting any other edge removes one vertex and one edge, so |E| − |V| + 1 and the total weight stay the same.

  2. Contraction preserves stability. When u and v merge, the new vertex has weight w(u) + w(v) and |r−1(u)| + |r−1(v)| − 2 half-edges, so for the new vertex 2w − 2 + |r−1| equals (2w(u) − 2 + |r−1(u)|) + (2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)|), a sum of two positive numbers. When a loop at v is contracted, 2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| is unchanged: the weight goes up by 1 and v loses 2 half-edges.

This is the reason for the weights. A vertex weight remembers loops that have been contracted.

Part 3

The moduli space of graphs

The graphs of Part 2 are combinatorial objects, so they cannot move continuously. To get a space, we give each edge a length that can vary.

Definition · metric graph

A metric stable graph is a pair (G, ℓ) where G is a connected stable graph of genus g with n marked legs and ℓ : E(G) → ℝ>0 assigns a positive length to each edge. Two metric graphs (G, ℓ) and (G′, ℓ′) are isomorphic if some isomorphism G → G′ carries ℓ to ℓ′. We say that G and (G, ℓ) have type (g, n).

Legs have no length: think of them as markers.

Now we work towards building a moduli space for metric stable graphs of genus g with n marked legs.

Step 1: the moduli space for one type of graph

Fix a stable graph G. To make a metric graph, we need to pick one length from (0, ∞) for each edge. So the possible length functions form the open orthant ℝE(G)>0, with one coordinate per edge. Two length functions give isomorphic metric graphs when an automorphism of G permutes the edges and carries one to the other. So the metric graphs with underlying graph G are parametrized by

ℝE(G)>0 / Aut(G).

Here is the graph with two parallel edges and legs 1 and 2 on different vertices. It has type (g, n) = (1, 2), and its automorphism swaps the two edges. So (ℓ1, ℓ2) and (ℓ2, ℓ1) give the same metric graph, and the quadrant of lengths folds in half along its diagonal.

Step 2: gluing the spaces together

Step 1 gives one space for each graph, and so far these spaces have nothing to do with each other. But they should be connected. When the length of an edge shrinks to 0, the metric graph looks more and more like the graph where that edge has been contracted. In a moduli space, moving continuously should deform the graph continuously, so a path along which ℓ(e) shrinks to 0 should arrive in the space of the contracted graph. This is just like the segments in Part 1, which approached the degenerate segment at the boundary point 0.

We build this in two moves. First, allow lengths to be 0, by closing up the orthant to ℝE(G)≥0. Second, decide which metric graph a point with ℓ(e) = 0 is: it should be the graph where e has shrunk away, the contraction G/e from Part 2. So we glue the face {ℓ(e) = 0} of the orthant of G to the orthant of G/e. In the figure above, each axis is a copy of the ray of the graph with one loop and both legs on its vertex, and the corner is the single vertex of weight 1.

Definition · the moduli space of graphs
Mg,n = ( ⊔G ℝE(G)≥0 / Aut(G) ) / ∼

The union runs over the stable graphs G of type (g, n), one from each isomorphism class. Each term ℝE(G)≥0 / Aut(G) is the space for the single graph G from Step 1, with lengths now allowed to be 0. The relation ∼ glues these spaces together by edge contraction: it identifies the face ℓ(e) = 0 in the space of G with the space of G/e.

  • As a set, the points of Mg,n are exactly the isomorphism classes of metric stable graphs of type (g, n). The graph with no edges, one vertex of weight g carrying all n legs, is a single cone point where every piece meets.
  • Each piece is a cone: scaling all the lengths of one of its points by a positive number gives another point of the same piece, so the piece is made of rays starting at the cone point. The pieces are indexed by graphs. A graph with k edges gives a k-dimensional piece, and contraction says which piece is a face of which. The graphs ordered by contraction form the poset drawn in the explorer.
  • By the edge bound in Part 2, the largest pieces have dimension 3g − 3 + n. They come from graphs with all weights 0 and all vertices trivalent.
  • Symmetry folds the space: a graph whose automorphisms permute its edges gives a folded cone.

The figure below puts all of this together for two small types. You can see every cone at once, and pick out any one of them.

Making it small enough to draw

Scaling all edge lengths by the same positive number t gives another metric graph of the same type, with the same underlying graph and every edge t times as long. So Mg,n is closed under scaling: whenever a metric graph (G, ℓ) is a point of the space, so is (G, tℓ) for every t > 0. As t runs from 0 to ∞, these points trace out a ray that starts at the cone point.

So we can scale every metric graph so that its total edge length is 1. Each ray meets the slice of graphs with total length 1 exactly once, so this slice still remembers the whole space: putting the rays back gives Mg,n again. We call the slice

Δg,n = { (G, ℓ) ∈ Mg,n : ∑e ℓ(e) = 1 }.

It has one dimension fewer, which makes it easier to picture, but the same overall structure. A graph with k edges now contributes a (k − 1)-simplex, the lengths that are positive and add up to 1, folded by its automorphisms, and the cone point disappears. So Δg,n has dimension 3g − 4 + n and is glued together from simplices. In the figures, the button “Rescale to total length 1” slides the point onto this slice, and the dashed outline in the big picture is Δ itself. All the pictures below draw Δg,n, except for the smallest ones, where the whole cone fits.

Part 4

Examples

In genus 0 every weight is 0 and every graph is a tree (|E| − |V| + 1 = 0). So a stable graph of type (0, n) is a tree whose leaves are the n labelled legs and whose vertices all have valence at least 3. Biologists call these phylogenetic trees, and M0,n is the space of phylogenetic trees on n labelled species. Every leg is labelled, so these trees have no nontrivial automorphisms and nothing gets folded.

M0,3: a point

The bound allows at most 3 · 0 − 3 + 3 = 0 edges. The only stable graph is one vertex with legs 1, 2, 3, so M0,3 is a single point.

M0,4: three rays

Now at most one edge is allowed. With no edges there is one vertex carrying legs 1, 2, 3, 4: the cone point. With one edge there are two trivalent vertices, and the legs split two and two. There are three ways to do that: 12|34, 13|24 and 14|23. Each gives a ray ℝ≥0 of edge lengths, and contracting the edge sends each ray's endpoint to the cone point. So M0,4 is three rays glued at a point, and Δ0,4 is three points. Is M0,4 a graph? Yes: a star K1,3 with infinitely long edges.

M0,5: the Petersen graph

Now dim M0,5 = 2, so Δ0,5 is 1-dimensional. It is a graph. Let's find its vertices and edges.

Vertices of Δ0,5 are trees with one edge. One edge splits the legs into two sides, and each side needs at least 2 legs so that both vertices are at least trivalent. So the split is a 2-subset against a 3-subset of {1, …, 5}. Label the tree by its 2-subset {i, j}. There are C(5, 2) = 10 of them.

Edges of Δ0,5 are trees with two edges. A trivalent tree with 5 legs has 3 internal vertices and 2 edges: a pair of legs at each end and one leg in the middle. This is a caterpillar. Contracting either edge gives a one-edge tree. If the ends hold the pairs {i, j} and {k, l}, the two contractions are the trees labelled {i, j} and {k, l}. To count them: 5 choices for the middle leg, then 3 ways to split the other four into two pairs, for 15 trees.

So Δ0,5 has a vertex for each 2-subset of {1, …, 5}, and {i, j} is adjacent to {k, l} exactly when the two subsets are disjoint. That is the Kneser graph K(5, 2), which is the Petersen graph. Before looking at the answer, try to label it yourself.

Each edge of Δ0,5 is a family of caterpillars. Walking along the edge from {i, j} to {k, l} shrinks one internal edge while the other grows. Drag the point to watch it happen.

Some checks.

  • Each vertex {i, j} has C(3, 2) = 3 disjoint partners, so the graph is 3-regular with 10 · 3 / 2 = 15 edges.
  • So M0,5 is the cone over the Petersen graph: 15 two-dimensional quadrants, glued in threes along 10 rays.
  • The cycle rank of the Petersen graph is 15 − 10 + 1 = 6 = 3!. In general Δ0,n is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of (n − 2)! spheres of dimension n − 4. For n = 5 that is 6 circles.

Examples with genus

M1,1: a ray. At most one edge is allowed. The graphs are a vertex of weight 1 with the leg, and a weight-0 vertex with a loop and the leg (it has 2 + 1 = 3 half-edges). Contracting the loop gives the first graph. The second graph has Aut = ℤ/2, which flips the loop's two half-edges, but the flip does not change the loop's length. So M1,1 is a ray and Δ1,1 is a point. This is the segment's endpoint swap from Part 1. The automorphism is invisible in the space, but it is still there, and it matters in algebraic geometry.

M1,2: a segment. At most two edges are allowed, so Δ1,2 is 1-dimensional. There are two graphs with two edges.

  • A: two weight-0 vertices joined by two parallel edges, with leg 1 on one vertex and leg 2 on the other. Aut(A) = ℤ/2 swaps the two edges, so its piece of Δ1,2 is the segment {ℓ1 + ℓ2 = 1} folded in half at ℓ1 = ℓ2 = ½. This is the slice of the folded quadrant from Part 3.
  • B: a vertex with a loop, joined by a bridge to a vertex carrying legs 1 and 2. Its automorphism flips the loop and fixes both lengths, so its piece is a full segment.

There are two graphs with one edge, the vertices of Δ1,2.

  • C: one weight-0 vertex with a loop and legs 1 and 2. It is A with either edge contracted, and B with the bridge contracted.
  • D: a weight-1 vertex joined by an edge to a vertex carrying legs 1 and 2. It is B with the loop contracted.

So Δ1,2 is a segment. The piece of B runs from D to C, and the folded piece of A continues from C to the point where ℓ1 = ℓ2, which is not glued to anything.

Activity: genus 2, no legs

List all stable graphs of type (2, 0) and organize them by contraction. There are 7. The bounds from Part 2 say each has at most 2 vertices and at most 3 edges. Try it before revealing the rows.

So Δ2 has two triangles, one for the theta graph and one for the dumbbell, each folded by its automorphisms. The theta graph's automorphisms permute its three edges in all six ways, so only a sixth of its triangle survives. The dumbbell's automorphisms swap its two loops, so half of its triangle survives. The two pieces are glued along the segment of figure-eights, also called roses.

Part 5

Why geometers care

This part is a story with pictures, not proofs. The combinatorics above is a shadow of something much bigger.

The moduli space of curves. Algebraic geometers study the moduli space of curves ℳg,n. Its points are Riemann surfaces of genus g (a sphere with g handles) with n marked points. It has complex dimension 3g − 3 + n, the same number as our bound on the number of edges. That is not a coincidence.

Degenerations and dual graphs. ℳg,n is not compact. A surface can degenerate when a loop drawn on it pinches down to a point. The limit is a nodal curve: several surfaces glued together at points. Every nodal curve has a dual graph.

Nodal curveDual graph
a componenta vertex
the genus of that componentthe weight of the vertex
a node (pinch point)an edge
a marked pointa leg

Two things line up exactly.

  • A nodal curve is stable, meaning it has only finitely many automorphisms, exactly when its dual graph is stable in our sense. The inequality 2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| > 0 says that each component, with its nodes and marked points removed, has negative Euler characteristic.
  • Contracting an edge corresponds to smoothing a node. So the way our cones glue is the way the boundary strata of the compactified moduli space fit together. A graph with k edges corresponds to a stratum of codimension k.

What the edge lengths mean. Roughly, the length of an edge measures how fast the corresponding neck pinches in a family of surfaces. A longer edge means a thinner neck.

The precise statement. The space Δg,n is the boundary complex of the Deligne–Mumford compactification of ℳg,n (Abramovich, Caporaso and Payne). As a result, its homology computes the top-weight cohomology of ℳg,n. Chan, Galatius and Payne used this to show that the rational cohomology of ℳg in degree 4g − 6 is nonzero for g = 3, 5 and every g ≥ 7, and that its dimension grows at least exponentially in g. Counting holes in a space of graphs told us something new about the space of all surfaces.

Back to M0,5. The compactified moduli space of 5 points on the sphere is a complex surface. Its boundary consists of 10 curves, one for each 2-subset of {1, …, 5}, and two of them meet exactly when their 2-subsets are disjoint. The Petersen graph is the picture of how they meet.

Everything from this semester shows up in current research on the geometry of surfaces: trees, cycles, contraction, automorphisms, and the Petersen graph.

For later

Exercises and further reading

Optional problems for anyone who wants more.

  1. Write the graph with one vertex, two loops and one leg in half-edge notation (V, H, r, ι). Compute |Aut(G)|.

    Answer

    |Aut(G)| = 8. Each loop can be flipped, and the two loops can be swapped: 2 · 2 · 2.

  2. Show that edge contraction preserves genus and stability.

    Hint

    See the two key facts at the end of Part 2: track |E| − |V| + 1, the weights, and 2w(v) − 2 + |r−1(v)| for the vertices involved.

  3. How many stable graphs of type (0, 6) have 3 edges?

    Answer

    105, the number of trivalent trees with 6 labelled leaves, which is (2 · 6 − 5)!! = 7 · 5 · 3.

  4. Show that Δ0,5 has no 3-cycles and no 4-cycles, so its girth is 5.

  5. The space Δ0,6 is 2-dimensional: it has a vertex for each tree with one edge, an edge for each tree with two edges, and a triangle for each trivalent tree. The link of a vertex is the graph with one point for each edge of Δ0,6 at that vertex, and a segment between two points for each triangle that contains both edges. Explain why the link of the vertex 56 | 1234 (the tree with one edge separating legs 5 and 6 from legs 1, 2, 3, 4) is the Petersen graph. You can see it in the explorer.

    Hint

    A tree that contracts to 56 | 1234 must split its vertex carrying legs 1, 2, 3, 4. That vertex has five half-edges: four legs and the edge towards legs 5 and 6. Splitting it is the same as choosing a point of Δ0,5, with the edge playing the role of a fifth leg. So the link is a copy of Δ0,5, which is the Petersen graph from Part 4.

  6. Verify the list of 7 stable graphs of type (2, 0), and compute |Aut| for the theta graph.

    Answer

    12. The symmetric group S3 permutes the three edges, and ℤ/2 swaps the two vertices.

  7. Challenge: show that the number of trivalent trees with n labelled leaves is (2n − 5)!!.
